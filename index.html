Our inaugural project

Thirteen weeks to give a child back their future.

Aisha is nine. A year ago she was in Primary 3 and could read her own name. Then her family fled their village. Today she can't remember the letters — and no school in the town she now lives in has room for her.

There are thousands of children like Aisha in Zamfara State right now. Not children who failed at school. Children whom school stopped being available to.

The GELI Catch-Up Learning Programme is our answer to them. It is a free, thirteen-week foundational learning programme in Hausa reading and early mathematics, delivered at weekends in community spaces — under a tree, in a ward centre, in a borrowed classroom — by facilitators from the children's own communities. Twenty-six lessons of thirty-five minutes each. No fees. No uniform. No entry test. No child turned away.

By week thirteen, a child who arrived unable to recognise a single letter can read simple Hausa sentences, count and write numbers to twenty, and add and subtract with confidence. More than that: they are back in the habit of learning, back among other children, and ready to be reintegrated into formal school.

About GELI

Who We Are

The Grassroots Empowerment and Livelihood Initiative (GELI) is an indigenous, community-based non-governmental organisation founded to close the widening intervention gap in Zamfara State, North-West Nigeria.

We were born out of a deep, first-hand understanding of the security, social and economic realities on the ground — not from a policy paper written elsewhere. GELI exists to ensure that development, opportunity and reliable information reach the communities that need them most, including those in local government areas that have become difficult or unsafe for external organisations to access.

We are built on a simple but powerful idea: the people best placed to serve Zamfara's communities are Zamfara's own sons and daughters. By recruiting and training qualified, educated indigenes from every local government area in the state, GELI provides a trusted local presence that national and international organisations, government agencies, donors, employers and commercial firms can rely on to deliver their programmes and reach their audiences safely, credibly and effectively.

Why GELI exists

Our Story — and the Need We Serve

Zamfara State is passing through one of the most challenging periods in its history. Persistent insecurity has forced large numbers of families to flee their rural communities for the relative safety of local government headquarters and urban centres. Many of these displaced families have lost their farms, their livelihoods and their stability. Their children, once enrolled in school, now roam the streets, out of education and often reduced to begging for food. This has produced a deepening education crisis across the state.

The consequences extend far beyond the classroom. As insecurity persists, the state's education, health, agriculture and broader economic systems continue to decline together. Families that once fed themselves and their communities can no longer farm; clinics and schools in affected areas struggle to function; and opportunity narrows for an entire generation of young people.

At the very moment when these communities most need support, many national and international organisations have reduced, suspended or withdrawn their activities — understandably reluctant to expose their staff to the risks of travelling to affected local government areas. The result is a widening intervention gap: the communities in greatest need have become the hardest for outside help to reach.

A quieter crisis: opportunity and information

Alongside insecurity sits a second, quieter crisis. Thousands of educated young people across Zamfara's local government areas are unemployed — not only because jobs are scarce, but because they never hear about the ones that exist. Federal and state empowerment schemes, loans, grants, skills programmes and recruitment drives are announced through channels that rarely reach the rural ward, and are too often mediated by middlemen who charge for access to something that is free. Employers in Gusau, Kaduna, Kano and Abuja report that they cannot find suitable candidates from these areas, while qualified indigenes sit at home unaware that recruitment is even open.

At the same time, digital tools — and now artificial intelligence — are rapidly reshaping what employers expect of a candidate. Young people who have never used a computer are being pushed further behind before they begin. And communities with no reliable source of information on contemporary issues are left exposed to rumour, misinformation and outright fraud.

Private sector

The commercial access gap

The same access problem constrains the private sector. Companies with products and services that would be genuinely useful to rural households — agricultural inputs, financial and mobile-money services, telecommunications, health and nutrition products, solar and building materials — cannot safely or credibly reach the hard-to-reach local government areas, and have no trusted local networks through which to introduce themselves. Communities lose access to goods, services and information; companies lose a market that would have paid for itself.

Our response

Our response

GELI was conceived as a direct, practical response to these gaps.

Where it is unsafe for outsiders to travel, we are already there. Where local knowledge and community trust are essential, we already have them. Where opportunity exists but news of it does not travel, we carry it.

By organising and training Zamfara's own qualified indigenes into a disciplined, professional workforce, GELI makes it possible for development, employment and legitimate commerce to continue — even in the places they had begun to leave behind.

Local knowledge. Local trust. Local action.

Compliance, safeguarding & information security

Compliance, Safeguarding and Information Security

GELI's value to any partner rests entirely on being trusted with two things: people and information. We therefore treat compliance not as paperwork attached to a contract, but as the condition of our licence to operate.

Every engagement — development, government or commercial — is delivered under the standards set out below. We actively welcome partner audit, verification and unannounced spot-checks against them.

Safeguarding: protecting the people we work with

Safeguarding takes precedence over programme delivery, over reputation and over commercial convenience. Our position is unambiguous.

Zero tolerance. GELI maintains zero tolerance of sexual exploitation, abuse and harassment (SEAH), of any form of abuse or exploitation of a child or adult at risk, and of the abuse of power by any member of personnel, associate, volunteer, promoter, consultant or downstream partner.

Recognised standards. We align with the IASC Six Core Principles relating to Protection from Sexual Exploitation and Abuse, and we adopt our partners' own safeguarding policies and reporting requirements where these are more stringent than our own.

Signed commitments before deployment. Every member of personnel signs the Code of Conduct and PSEAH commitment before any contact with a community, receives induction training on it, and repeats that training annually.

Child safeguarding. No unsupervised one-to-one contact with children; a two-adult rule for all activities involving children; written parent or guardian consent for participation and for any photography or filming; and adherence to the Child Rights Act and applicable state child protection provisions.

Safer recruitment. Identity and address verification, credential checks, a specific safeguarding reference, disclosure of any prior disciplinary or misconduct history, and verification of community standing — applied to field promoters and short-term enumerators exactly as to permanent staff.

A named focal point — and a woman to report to. A designated Safeguarding Focal Point independent of programme delivery, with an alternate of the opposite gender, so that women and girls always have a woman to whom they can report.

Accessible reporting channels. Multiple confidential routes — a dedicated telephone line, the named focal point, a written complaints form and an anonymous channel — publicised in Hausa and English in every community where we operate, in formats accessible to people who cannot read.

Survivor-centred response. The safety, wishes, confidentiality, dignity and non-discrimination of the survivor guide every decision. Immediate referral to medical, psychosocial and legal support where wanted, and no pressure on a survivor to report to authorities.

Investigation and notification. A documented investigation, disciplinary and referral procedure, with notification to the affected partner within the timeframe their contract specifies — 24 or 48 hours as required — and referral to the appropriate authorities where the law requires it.

Non-retaliation. Anyone reporting a concern in good faith is protected from retaliation of any kind. Retaliation is itself a disciplinary offence.

Do no harm. A conflict-sensitivity and do-no-harm assessment before entering any community, so that our presence does not expose participants, personnel or the community to risk.

Third-party data: we are custodians, never owners

GELI routinely handles data that belongs to somebody else — beneficiary records for a development partner, applicant records for an employer, consumer data for a commercial client, enumeration data for a government agency. Our standing position is that we are custodians of that data and never its owners.

Lawful basis and purpose limitation. Personal data is collected only where there is a lawful basis, and used only for the specific purpose agreed in writing with the data owner. Nothing else.

Informed consent. Participants are told, in a language they genuinely understand, what is being collected, why, who will see it, how long it will be held and how to withdraw. Consent is recorded. For children, consent is obtained from a parent or guardian together with the child's own assent.

Data minimisation. We collect the least data necessary for the agreed purpose. Sensitive personal data — health, biometric, religious, political or similar — is not collected without explicit written instruction and justification from the data owner.

Legal framework. We comply with the Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA) 2023 and the directions of the Nigeria Data Protection Commission, and with the UK/EU GDPR or other regimes where a partner's obligations extend to them.

Written data processing agreements. Every engagement involving personal data is governed by a data processing agreement or equivalent contractual clause specifying ownership, permitted use, security standards, sub-processing, retention, breach notification, and return or destruction on exit.

Binding confidentiality undertakings. Every person with access to third-party data signs a confidentiality and non-disclosure undertaking that survives the end of their engagement with GELI.

No secondary use, ever. Partner and client data is never reused for another engagement, shared with a third party, sold, or applied to GELI's own purposes. Recruitment data is never shown to a competing employer; one commercial client's market data is never shared with another.

Segregation and need-to-know access. Data belonging to different partners is stored separately, under access rights granted strictly on a need-to-know basis, and access is logged.

Retention and disposal. Data is retained only for the agreed period, then securely deleted or returned to the owner, with a signed certificate of destruction where the partner requires one.

Data subject rights. Requests for access, correction, objection or erasure are logged on receipt and referred to the data owner for instruction within the agreed timeframe.

Imagery and case studies. Photographs, film and personal stories are collected and used only with specific, separate, revocable written consent — and never in a way that could place a person at risk.

Information security

Our controls are designed to be proportionate to a field organisation working across fourteen local government areas, and to withstand a partner's due-diligence review.

Access control — unique named accounts, strong passwords enforced through a password manager, and multi-factor authentication on all email, cloud storage and data-collection platforms. Shared logins are prohibited.

Device security — full-disk encryption, enforced screen locks, current operating systems and anti-malware on every laptop and tablet, with remote wipe enabled on all mobile devices used for field data collection.

Encryption in transit and at rest — digital data collection on encrypted, authenticated platforms; paper forms transported in sealed envelopes and held in locked storage until digitisation, then securely destroyed.

Approved channels only — personal email accounts, personal cloud storage, consumer messaging applications and personal USB drives are prohibited for third-party data. Transfers use only the channel and platform the partner specifies.

Least privilege and prompt revocation — access rights are role-based, reviewed quarterly, and revoked the same day a person leaves or changes role.

AI tool governance — we teach AI literacy and we use AI tools, which makes us explicit about the risk. Personal, confidential or partner-identifiable data is never entered into a public AI assistant or any third-party tool not covered by an approved agreement. Approved AI use is confined to non-sensitive drafting, learning and analysis, and all AI-assisted output is reviewed by a person before use.

Backup and continuity — encrypted backups on an agreed schedule, held separately from primary storage, with periodic restore testing and a documented continuity plan for loss of premises, power or connectivity.

Incident response — a documented breach response procedure: immediate containment, assessment of risk to affected individuals, notification of the affected partner within the contractual window and of the Nigeria Data Protection Commission within the statutory 72-hour period where applicable, remediation, and a written post-incident report with corrective actions.

Physical security — locked offices and filing cabinets, controlled visitor access, clear-desk practice, and secure shredding of paper records.

Training — information security and data protection induction before deployment, and refresher training at least annually, with completion recorded.

Assurance, audit and partner verification

A documented policy suite, kept current — Safeguarding and PSEAH; Child Protection; Data Protection and Privacy; Information Security and Acceptable Use; Code of Conduct; Whistleblowing; Anti-Fraud, Bribery and Corruption; Conflict of Interest; and Complaints and Feedback.

Due diligence as routine — we complete partner capacity assessments, due-diligence questionnaires and policy reviews as a normal part of contracting, not as an exception.

Open to audit — partners and clients may audit our records, systems, sites and personnel files relating to their engagement, on reasonable notice.

Annual self-assessment — a yearly internal assessment against safeguarding and data-protection standards, with findings and a corrective action plan made available to partners on request.

Financial accountability — segregated project accounting, full narrative and financial reporting to the partner's format, and independent audit of the organisation's accounts.

GELI'S SELF-FUNDED COMMUNITY PROGRAMME

Our Self-Sponsored Training and Public Awareness Programme

GELI's management funds and delivers — at no cost whatsoever to participants — a continuing programme of training, orientation and public awareness for young people and community members across Zamfara State.

This programme is not donor-dependent and is not contingent on winning a contract. It is financed by the organisation's management as a deliberate investment in the state's people, and it runs whether or not a partner project is in place.

It exists because the gap it addresses is one nobody has been paid to close. Opportunity is announced in places our communities cannot hear it. Government interventions are wrapped in procedure that nobody explains. The labour market has gone digital while whole wards have never touched a keyboard. And the issues shaping daily life are discussed everywhere except where their consequences land hardest.

The programme rests on four pillars.

01
Pillar 1 — Job Opportunity Awareness

Making visible the employment, apprenticeship and self-employment opportunities that exist in and beyond the state, and equipping participants to compete for them.

Participants gain: knowledge of live vacancies, recruitment cycles and employer expectations; CV and application writing; interview preparation; credential and documentation readiness; how and where to search safely; and how to recognise and refuse recruitment scams and "pay-for-a-job" fraud.

02
Pillar 2 — Federal Government Programmes and Interventions

Explaining, in plain Hausa and English, the federal and state programmes, loans, grants, stipends, skills schemes and recruitment drives that participants are entitled to apply for.

Participants gain: a practical understanding of what each programme offers, who qualifies, which documents are required, how and when to register, where to verify authenticity, and how to bypass the middlemen and impostors who charge for access to free schemes.

03
Pillar 3 — Basic Computer and Artificial Intelligence (AI) Literacy

Foundational digital skills and an honest, practical first introduction to AI tools — designed for people who may never have used a computer.

Participants gain: keyboard, file and folder basics; word processing, spreadsheets and presentations; email, internet search and safe browsing; smartphone productivity; how to use AI assistants responsibly for writing, learning, job search and small business; and a clear-eyed grounding in online safety, privacy, misinformation and the limits of AI.

04
Pillar 4 — Public Orientation and Awareness of Contemporary Issues

Helping communities understand and respond to the issues shaping their lives right now.

Participants gain: civic responsibility and rights; peace, security consciousness and community cohesion; the value of education and of keeping children in school; health, hygiene and nutrition awareness; substance abuse prevention; climate and environmental change; digital fraud and misinformation awareness; and the participation of women and girls in community and economic life.

How the programme is delivered

1

In Hausa and English, by GELI personnel drawn from the participants' own local government areas, so instruction arrives in a familiar voice.

2

In accessible community venues — schools, ward centres, skills acquisition centres, and religious and traditional gathering places — so that participation costs nothing but time.

3

Completely free of charge — no tuition, no registration fee, no "form fee", no material fee and no condition of any kind.

4

Open to young men and women alike, with deliberate outreach to girls, out-of-school youth, persons with disabilities and displaced people.

5

Computer and AI literacy delivered using GELI's own equipment and, where power or connectivity is limited, in offline-capable formats.

6

Structured with attendance records, pre- and post-session assessment and follow-up, so that impact can be evidenced rather than assumed.

7

Under the same safeguarding, consent and data-protection standards as every other GELI activity, including written consent for participation and for the use of photographs.

Why we fund it ourselves. Three reasons. It is the right thing to do, and it needed doing. It builds and continually refreshes the talent pool that our recruitment clients draw on — which means the young person we train today is the candidate we can credibly present tomorrow. And it earns our field teams the standing in the community that makes every other kind of work possible, because our relationship with a community does not begin and end with a project cycle.

Why this programme exists

A generation is losing its place in the classroom.

Across Zamfara State, insecurity has emptied villages. Families who farmed the same land for generations have walked away from it, arriving in local government headquarters and urban centres with whatever they could carry. What they could not carry was their children's education.

The school is still standing in the village they left. But they are not in the village any more.

So the children wait. Some for a term. Some for a year. Some for longer than anyone intended. And with every month that passes, what they had already learned quietly drains away — the letters, the numbers, the confidence, the habit of sitting down and concentrating. A child who could read her own name in Primary 3 cannot recognise a single letter eighteen months later. That is not a failure of the child. It is what happens when learning simply stops.

And here is the cruelty of it: by the time a place is finally found for that child in a school, she has fallen so far behind her age group that the classroom becomes a humiliation rather than an opportunity. Many drop out within weeks. Some never enrol at all. Girls are lost first, and lost fastest.

“These are not children who failed at school. They are children whom school stopped being available to.”

The gap nobody was funding

A displaced child does not need a full curriculum to start again. She needs a bridge — a short, focused, achievable programme that rebuilds the foundations she has lost, restores her confidence, and hands her back to the formal school system ready to keep up.

That bridge did not exist in Zamfara at any scale. Formal schools are stretched beyond capacity and cannot run remedial streams. Most donor programmes cannot reach the affected LGAs. And the families themselves have nothing left to pay with.

So GELI built it. And GELI is funding it.

The Programme

What the Catch-Up Learning Programme is

The GELI Catch-Up Learning Programme is a free, thirteen-week foundational learning programme for children whose education has been interrupted by conflict, displacement or disadvantage.

It teaches two subjects — Hausa literacy and foundational mathematics — through twenty-six carefully sequenced lessons of thirty-five minutes each, delivered two lessons per weekend so that no child has to choose between the programme and whatever else their family needs from them.

It runs where the children already are: in ward centres, community spaces, borrowed classrooms, mosque and church halls, and under trees. It is taught in Hausa, in the children's own first language, by facilitators recruited from the children's own communities and trained by GELI over four full days.

Thirteen weeks, not thirteen months

Short enough to finish. Long enough to change something. A child who completes the programme has a visible, dated achievement — and so does the family who sent her.

Thirty-five focused minutes

Every lesson follows the same six-part rhythm, so children always know what is coming next. Predictability is not a limitation here — for a child who has lost her routine, it is the point.

Taught in Hausa, by neighbours

The facilitator is not a stranger flown in for a project cycle. She is from the ward. Parents know her family. That is why children are allowed to come.

Completely free

No tuition. No registration. No "form fee". No uniform requirement. No material fee. No condition of any kind. Funded by GELI's own management.

Open to every child

Girls and boys equally, with deliberate outreach to girls, to children with disabilities, and to the children of displaced families. No entrance test. No child turned away.

Safeguarded from the first day

Two adults present at every session. No unsupervised one-to-one contact. Written parent or guardian consent for participation and for any photograph. A named focal point in every community.

The Method

How it works: a method built for Hausa, not translated into it

Most reading programmes that reach Northern Nigeria are English-language methods with Hausa words dropped into them. They teach children to sound out isolated consonants — "nnn", "kkk" — because that is how English phonics works.

Hausa does not work that way. And our programme was designed around that fact from the first page.

The literacy strand — the abakada spine

In the Hausa abakada system, the name of a consonant is that consonant plus a: n is "na", k is "ka", ts is "tsa".

The letter name is therefore already a readable syllable. And two syllables make a word.

na + na = Nana

ka + ka = kaka

This is why children on our programme read real Hausa words within the first fortnight — not nonsense drills, not English-shaped sounds, but their own grandmother's name. It is a moment facilitators are trained to stop and mark, because it is usually the moment a child decides she can do this after all.

The programme teaches the vowels first (a e i o u), then introduces consonants in carefully chosen pairs — n k, then m b, then l g, then s t — with a review week at the halfway point and another at the end. Every word bank is strictly cumulative: a child is never asked to read a word containing a letter she has not yet been taught. Nothing is set up to fail.

Week 6

Muna nan

"We are here."

Week 13

Muna zuwa makaranta

"We are going to school."

The last sentence a child reads on this programme is a sentence about going back to school. That is not an accident.

The numeracy strand — quantity before symbol

The mathematics strand mirrors the literacy strand exactly: same thirteen weeks, same twenty-six lessons, same thirty-five-minute frame, same children, same facilitators.

It is governed by two unbreakable rules:

1

Real objects, then pictures, then the written numeral. Always in that order. Never start a child with the abstract symbol alone.

2

Always teach the quantity and the numeral together. Never a numeral in isolation. This is the single most important rule in the guide.

The sequence moves from counting and one-to-one correspondence to twenty, through comparing and ordering, numeral formation and number bonds within five, addition within five and then ten, teen numbers — explicitly linked to the Hausa goma sha X ("ten-and-X") construction that makes teen numbers logical rather than arbitrary — then subtraction, fact families, and finally addition and subtraction within twenty with one-step word problems drawn from the market, the household and the farm.

By the final week, a child who arrived unable to count reliably to ten can count, read and write numbers to twenty, compare quantities, and add and subtract within twenty using problems that sound like her own life.

Notice where the time goes. The longest segment is not instruction — it is play. This is deliberate. These children have been out of school for months or years. Many associate learning with failure, and several have experienced things no child should. A programme that opens with a song, spends a third of its time playing, and closes with a story is not a soft programme — it is a programme designed by people who understand that a frightened child does not learn.

And every single game carries an explicit "Adapting for mixed ages" note, because a catch-up class is never one age group. A seven-year-old and a twelve-year-old will sit in the same circle. Both must leave having been stretched.

The Facilitators

The people who will teach it

A programme is only ever as good as the person standing in front of the circle.

GELI recruits facilitators from the very communities the classes will serve — educated indigenes who already live in the ward, speak the language natively, and are known to the families whose children will attend. That last point is not sentimentality. In a community that has learned to be cautious about strangers, a facilitator whose family is known is the difference between a child being allowed to attend and a child being kept at home.

Facilitator Training

Facilitator training session in progress

Every facilitator completes a four-day residential training before they teach a single lesson:

Days 1–2 — Literacy

The six-segment lesson frame; the abakada method and why isolated consonant sounds are prohibited; story selection; the full fourteen-game Literacy Games Bank, walked through and practised; attendance recording; learner mobilisation and how to run a parent sensitisation meeting; a ninety-five-minute safeguarding block; and a full lesson simulation with structured peer feedback.

Days 3–4 — Numeracy

The numeracy lesson frame and its two governing rules; the fourteen-game Numeracy Games Bank; the counting, addition and subtraction chants; the physical kit — counters, number cards, ten-frame, number line; supporting struggling learners; adapting word problems across mixed ages; a numeracy-specific safeguarding refresher; a second full lesson simulation; and the logistics of running both subjects week by week.

Safeguarding

Safeguarding: the non-negotiables

This programme brings adults into contact with children who are, by definition, among the most vulnerable in the state — displaced, out of school, and in many cases separated from the adults who used to protect them.

We treat that responsibility as coming before everything else, including the programme itself.

1

Two adults, always.

A two-adult rule applies to every session. There is no unsupervised one-to-one contact with a child, at any time, for any reason.

2

Written consent, twice over.

Written parent or guardian consent is required for a child's participation, and a separate, specific, revocable written consent is required before any photograph or film is taken. Photographs are never used in a way that could place a child at risk.

3

Safer recruitment for every facilitator.

Identity and address verification, credential checks, a specific safeguarding reference, disclosure of any prior disciplinary history, and confirmation of standing in the candidate's own community.

4

Signed before contact.

Every facilitator signs GELI's Code of Conduct and PSEAH commitment before meeting a single child, and completes safeguarding training as part of the four-day course.

5

A named focal point — and a woman to report to.

Every community where the programme runs has a named Safeguarding Focal Point, with an opposite-gender alternate, so that girls always have a woman they can go to.

6

Reporting channels children can actually use.

Confidential routes publicised in Hausa and English, in formats that work for people who cannot read.

7

Story selection rules.

Even the Storytime segment is governed: facilitators are prohibited from selecting stories involving violence, fleeing, fire or the loss of a parent. These children have lived those stories. The programme does not re-tell them.

Safeguarding takes precedence over programme delivery, over reputation and over convenience. If those two things ever conflict, safeguarding wins.

What thirteen weeks will change

For the child

She arrives having lost the letters. She leaves reading simple Hausa sentences aloud, counting and writing to twenty, and adding and subtracting with confidence.

But the measurable part is not the whole of it. She also leaves having sat still in a circle for thirteen weeks, having answered a question in front of other children and been right, having been expected somewhere every weekend. For a child whose year has been defined by what was taken from her, being expected somewhere is not a small thing.

Most importantly, she leaves ready to be reintegrated into formal school — not as the child who is embarrassingly behind, but as a child who can keep up.

For the family

A displaced family carries a specific kind of grief about their children's education: the sense that whatever else they salvage, this particular thing is gone. The programme returns it, at no cost, without asking the family for anything they do not have.

It also brings parents into the picture deliberately. Facilitators run a sensitisation meeting before classes begin, and parents are given a clear account of what their child will learn and when. Parents who see progress in thirteen weeks re-enrol children in formal school. That is the real prize.

For the community

Every class is taught by someone from that community, using materials in that community's language, in a space that community provided. The programme is not something done to a community — it is something the community hosts. Traditional and religious leaders are engaged before the first lesson, not informed after it.

And each class creates paid, dignified work for an educated young indigene who might otherwise be sitting at home.

For Zamfara State

Every child returned to formal school is one child fewer in the out-of-school statistic that will define this state's next twenty years. Thirteen weeks is a short intervention. Not going to school is a life sentence.
